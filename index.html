AEI · GRADE 11 ENGLISH PROJECT

THE UNREAD PAGES

Some thoughts are too loud to say out loud.

What would you find if you could read a teenager’s mind?

A diary is where a teenager says what the day would not allow. Fear of disappointing a parent, a message left on “seen”, a crush never mentioned aloud: these are hard to say to a face, so they are written down. This project follows the six themes most likely to fill those pages. The entries are fictional, written for this project. The notes beside them are factual commentary.

Sunday 11 May, 11:48 pm

Chemistry test on Tuesday. I have opened the same chapter four times and read the same paragraph four times.

Mama asked at dinner how school was. I said “fine.” It isn’t, but fine is easier than watching her face change. Baba keeps saying he only wants my best. I believe him. That’s the problem. I don’t know what my best is supposed to look like.

Tomorrow I’ll start properly. (I said that yesterday.)

THE FIRST PAGE

School pressure

Exams, grades and family expectations take up much of a teenager’s week, so they take up much of the diary. Writers rarely describe the work itself. They describe the fear around it: procrastination as avoidance, and a confident face worn for everyone else.

Tuesday 20 May, 4:15 pm

Rana left my message on “seen” for three hours. Then she posted a story with Lina.

The group chat moved on to a joke I didn’t understand, so I sent a laughing emoji anyway. Nobody noticed. That’s the strange part: I’m in the chat and still feel like I’m outside it.

Maybe she was busy. Maybe I’m the one who changed.

THE SECOND PAGE

Friendship and belonging

At this age, friends often matter more than anyone else’s opinion. Group chats make closeness visible, and so they make exclusion visible too. Misunderstandings that once faded by tomorrow now sit on a screen with a timestamp.

Friday 30 May, 9:02 pm

I said “hi” to him today. Just hi. My voice came out like it belonged to someone else.

He smiled and asked about the Arabic homework. We talked for ninety seconds. I have replayed it two hundred times and found three things I should have said.

If he doesn’t feel the same, I’ll survive. I think. Probably.

THE THIRD PAGE

Love and crushes

A first crush is a new and intense feeling with no instructions. Diaries hold what is too risky to say aloud: the replayed conversation, the hope, the fear of rejection. Writing it down makes it easier to look at calmly.

Monday 9 June, 1:10 am

I scrolled for an hour and ended up knowing everyone’s best day and my own worst one.

I know they edit those photos. I know. It still works on me. Last year I liked different music and a different laugh. Which one was real?

Maybe the real me is just the one I haven’t finished building. Ask: who decided I was done?

THE FOURTH PAGE

Identity and insecurity

Adolescence is when people ask who they are. Social media adds constant comparison, and the diary becomes a place to try out versions of the self without an audience.

Saturday 21 June, 6:20 am

I woke up early and watched the sun reach the wall. I want to build something that is mine. I don’t fully know what yet.

My parents hope for something safe. I understand why. They have seen how hard things can get. But I’m scared that safe and mine are two different roads.

I might fail. I’d rather fail at something I chose.

THE FIFTH PAGE

Dreams and the future

Teenagers write about careers, independence and family hopes together, because the future feels like one big decision. Fear of failure and ambition sit side by side, and the diary is where both are allowed.

Thursday 3 July, 2:30 am

Awake again. My mind keeps running conversations that never happened.

At school I was loud and everyone laughed. At home I said “I’m tired.” Nobody asked what kind of tired. I’m not sure I would have known how to answer.

I think I need someone to ask twice. Maybe tomorrow I’ll tell Mama something real.

THE SIXTH PAGE

Thoughts nobody hears

Many teenagers show one face in public and feel another in private. That gap is normal, but it can feel lonely. Privacy is healthy, and so is eventually sharing the heavier thoughts. If they stay heavy, talk to a parent, teacher, counselor or someone you trust.

THE THINGS WE NEVER SAY

I wish people knew how hard I try.

These are fictional thoughts written for this project. They are not real student submissions.

A diary is more than a record of the day.

It is where a teenager works out identity, sorts out feelings, protects hopes, and makes sense of growing up. The pressure, the friendships, the crushes, the comparison, the dreams and the quiet nights are different pages of one story. Every teenager has an inner world that others do not always see. The diary gives that world a voice.

A diary is where a teenager is allowed to be unfinished.
